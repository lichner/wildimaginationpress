Most of the people who visit this site are not in Australia. Our visitor numbers tell us a lot of you are reading from the United States and Germany, so there is a fair chance the Australian bush is something you have seen in a documentary rather than somewhere you have actually stood. I thought it was about time I showed you around properly, at the height a seven-year-old sees it from.

## First, the bush is not the outback

People mix these two up all the time, and I understand why, but the difference is worth getting right.

The outback is the dry red middle of the country. It is vast and hot and mostly empty, and it is almost certainly what you picture when someone says Australia.

The bush is something else entirely. It is the scrubby, wooded, unfarmed country that begins at the edge of perfectly ordinary suburbs: gum trees, fallen bark, dry leaf litter that crunches underfoot, and birds you hear long before you see them. Plenty of Australian kids live ten minutes from bush they can walk into after school, which is why it turns up in our childhoods roughly where the woods sit for children in northern Europe, or the creek behind the houses for kids in the American Midwest.

On a hot day the whole thing smells of eucalyptus oil. The documentaries never mention that part.

## Children build things there

Ask an Australian adult about their childhood and sooner or later you will hear about a cubby.

A cubby house is our word for the shelter kids build themselves. Americans would say fort or den, and our German readers might be thinking of a Bude or a Baumhaus. A bush cubby is made from whatever happens to be lying around: leaning branches, sheets of bark, rocks dragged into a circle, maybe a bent sapling for a doorway. No adult supervises it and nobody buys a kit, which is exactly the point. The child decides where the walls go.

The part I find fascinating is how quickly a cubby stops being a shelter and turns into a place. First it gets a name, then rules, then a border, and before long there is a neighbouring cubby to negotiate with. Give four children a week of school holidays and a patch of bush and they will not come back having built a hut. They will come back having built a town, complete with a government.

If you have read *Bushlandia*, you already know that is exactly where the story came from.

## The bird that copies everything

The superb lyrebird lives in the wet forests of Australia's southeast, and it is honestly one of the strangest animals we have.

It is about a metre long, which puts it among the largest songbirds in the world, yet it barely flies. It spends its life on the forest floor raking through leaf litter, and it often stays in the same small patch of forest for its entire life.

What makes it truly odd is the singing. Roughly 70 to 80 percent of what a male lyrebird sings is not his own song at all. It is imitation, mostly of other birds and occasionally of marsupials, and the copies are so accurate that the birds being imitated cannot always tell the difference.

You may have seen the famous David Attenborough footage of a lyrebird doing a chainsaw and a camera shutter. Those particular birds were captive ones, which is worth knowing before you repeat the story at a dinner party. Wild lyrebirds copy whatever is actually around them, and their repertoire depends on which patch of forest they grew up in. Each lyrebird is, in its way, a recording of its own neighbourhood, and if the neighbourhood changes, so does the song.

Once I learned that, I knew I had found the right guardian for a story about children who damage the place they love.

Depending on your strategy, that makes it either a bedtime book or a way to wear a toddler out before bedtime. Twenty four pages, five to ten minutes, longer once the requests for a repeat start.

## Three things to try, wherever you live

None of this needs Australian bush. Woods, a park or a decent backyard will do fine.

1. Build with only what is already on the ground: nothing cut, nothing bought, just what has fallen. The restriction sounds annoying and turns out to be the entire fun of it.

2. Play lyrebird. Everyone closes their eyes for two minutes and counts the separate sounds they can hear, then each person picks one and has a go at copying it. Children are far better at this than adults, and they love finding that out.

3. Give the place a name. A named place gets defended. I have watched that one small move teach a five-year-old more about looking after nature than any lecture ever managed.
